When the year is busy

Two distinct rush seasons, and neither is the one most people name. The index sets each month against the all-years daily average.

May and June run hottest — school holidays, not a festival. The Brahmotsavam season in September–October reads only slightly above average on counts, because throughput is capped by how many people the queue can physically move; the crowd shows up in waiting hours instead (section 05).

05

Crowd against queue

Each bulletin reports yesterday’s pilgrim count alongside this morning’s queue — “Present Situation on 17-08-2026”. So the queue reading is re-dated to the morning it describes before being paired with that day’s eventual total; 3,608 days carry both. Dashed lines mark the medians.

festival or national holiday ordinary day

More waiting does not mean more darshans. Beyond roughly 70,000 a day the cloud goes vertical — the queue lengthens while the count barely moves. That is a hard capacity ceiling, not a demand signal.

Read the pairing carefully: the queue figure is a single morning observation, and the count is a whole day’s throughput. They describe the same calendar day but not the same instant, so this shows how a morning’s queue relates to that day’s outturn — it is not a per-pilgrim waiting time, and dividing one by the other would not mean anything.

07

What festivals actually do

451 days carry a major festival or national holiday against 3,956 ordinary days.

Festival days average 64,526 pilgrims against 63,824 on ordinary days — a rise of just 1.1%. The queue tells the real story: 11.8 hours of waiting on festival days against 11.1 on ordinary ones.

The temple does not serve many more people on a festival; the same number wait considerably longer. During Brahmotsavams TTD suspends paid and privileged darshan streams, which lengthens the free queue without raising total throughput.

Festival dating comes from TTD’s own dated posts, not from a generic almanac. Coverage is strong for Brahmotsavams and fixed national holidays and thinner for single-day lunar festivals such as Vaikunta Ekadasi, which the news titles rarely state as a date — so treat the festival column as a floor, not a census.

How this was built. All 6,614 posts in the darshan category were pulled through the site’s WordPress REST API, then each post’s pilgrim figure and date were read from its title and body. The bulletin format changed four times in thirteen years, so the parser handles each era explicitly — including the 2013–2019 practice of publishing an interim “3am to 6pm” count alongside the next morning’s full-day total. Interim snapshots are held separately and never mixed into a daily total.

One correction worth stating. An earlier cut of this page paired each day’s pilgrim count with the queue length printed in the same bulletin. That was wrong: a bulletin reports yesterday’s count but this morning’s queue, and where the bulletin states the situation date it matches the publication date in 938 of 959 posts. Queue readings are now re-dated to the morning they describe. A ratio of pilgrims to queue-hours was also dropped — dividing a full day’s throughput by a single morning’s queue estimate does not measure anything.

The dataset was checked against the rendered listing pages independently: 174 posts sampled across pages 1 to 662 matched on link, title and figure with no discrepancies. Where two bulletins reported the same date, an identical figure was kept once and a disagreement resolved to the higher value. Nothing on this page is estimated — every value appeared verbatim in a TTD bulletin, and known source defects (mis-grouped digits such as 67,3574, truncated titles) are listed in the accompanying anomalies file rather than silently corrected.
